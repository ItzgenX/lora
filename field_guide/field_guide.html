The Conditional LoRA Field Guide

How CTRLorALTer / LoRAdapter steers Stable Diffusion 1.5 — built up from "what is a neural network" all the way to why your SegFormer segmentation swap barely touches the architecture. Each lesson pairs a plain-English concept with the exact code that implements it.

Order matters. Each lesson leans on the one before it. Two panel types: ▍concept = the idea, no code. ▍in this repo = the real file. Goal: CARLA segmentation map in → real-world-style driving image out.
01Foundation

Numbers in, numbers out

Concept

To a computer, a photo is nothing but a grid of numbers. A 512×512 photo has 3 numbers per pixel (how much red, green, and blue) — 512 × 512 × 3 = 786,432 numbers, arranged in a grid.

A neural network is, underneath everything, a big function: numbers go in one end, a lot of multiply-and-add arithmetic happens in the middle (using millions of internal numbers the network learned), and numbers come out the other end. "Training" just means: show the network many examples, and nudge those internal numbers so its outputs get a little closer to what you wanted, over and over.

Every idea in this guide — VAE, UNet, LoRA, the mapper — is exactly this pattern, "numbers in, numbers out," just arranged cleverly. Keep that in your head; it de-mystifies everything below. You never need to picture anything mystical happening — only ever: some grid of numbers goes into a box, a different grid of numbers comes out.

02Foundation

What "latent space" actually is

Concept

786,432 numbers per image is a lot to push through a network thousands of times during training — slow, and memory-hungry. So: train one separate, smaller network, once, whose only job is compression — squeeze a photo down into a much smaller grid of numbers that still captures "what this photo looks like," well enough that a second small network can expand it back into (almost) the original photo.

Think of it like describing a face to a sketch artist with a short list of dials instead of handing over a full photograph: "roundness of face: 0.7, eye spacing: 0.3, smile amount: 0.9, lighting: warm..." A short list of numbers, but a meaningful one — change one dial slightly and you get a slightly different, still-plausible face. That short list is a compressed description, not a random scramble.

That compressed grid is called a latent ("hidden" representation). The word "space" gets added because these compressed descriptions aren't scattered randomly — photos that look similar end up as nearby points in this compressed number-grid, the same way similar addresses cluster together on a map. Latent space = a compressed, organized coordinate system for images.

The network that learns this compression is called a VAE (Variational Autoencoder). It's really two halves: an encoder (photo → latent) and a decoder (latent → photo). One crucial detail for later: the latent isn't one flat list of dials — it's still a grid, just a much smaller one. Top-left of the latent grid still roughly corresponds to the top-left of the photo. It's a compressed picture, not a compressed caption.

In this repo

Read from src/model.py — SD15.get_input(), decorated @torch.no_grad() so no gradients ever flow through the VAE during training:

latents = self.vae.encode(imgs).latent_dist.sample()
latents = latents * self.vae.config.scaling_factor

The real UNet config on disk (checkpoints/local_models/stable-diffusion-v1-5/unet/config.json) says "sample_size": 64 — confirming the latent really is a 64×64 grid, exactly as drawn above. This one number is the seed for Lesson 4.

03Foundation

Diffusion: destroy the image, then learn to reverse it

Concept

Take a real photo's latent grid (Lesson 2). Add a little random static to it. Add more. Keep going until it's pure static — nothing recognizable left. That's the forward process, and it isn't learned — it's just arithmetic, you decide how much noise to add at each of many steps.

Now train a network to do the reverse: given a noisy latent and "how noisy is this, roughly," guess what noise was just added. If it can always guess correctly, you can subtract that guess — one small step at a time — and walk backwards from pure static to a real image. Generation = start from pure random static and run that reverse process for ~50 steps.

This is the entire idea of a diffusion model. The network that makes the guess is the UNet — the single most important box in this whole guide, and the one everything else in this repo modifies.

In this repo

SD15.forward(), src/model.py:473 — the "add noise, guess it, compare" loop, one training step:

noisy_latents = self.noise_scheduler.add_noise(latents, noise, timesteps)
model_pred = self.unet(noisy_latents, timesteps,
                        encoder_hidden_states=prompt_embeds).sample
loss = F.mse_loss(model_pred, noise)   # how wrong was the guess?

That's it — that's the training loop's entire mathematical core. Everything from here on is about what goes into that self.unet(...) call, and how the UNet's own layers get modified so its guess can be steered by a segmentation map.

04Architecture

The UNet's shape

Concept

Picture a funnel: the network first shrinks the 64×64 latent grid repeatedly (a coarser, more "global" view each step), then expands it back the same number of times (more precise again). Drawn out, it looks like the letter U — hence "UNet." Each shrink/expand step is a resolution stage.

The shrink side and the expand side are cross-wired with skip connections — fine detail computed on the way down gets handed straight across to the matching stage on the way up, so the network doesn't have to re-invent detail it already had.

Why this matters for us: every place we can later attach a conditioning signal exists at one specific resolution stage. A signal meant for the coarse, deep stage means something different than one meant for the detailed, shallow stage — so the conditioning has to be produced at all 4 sizes, matched up correctly. That's the whole reason a "mapper" network exists (Lesson 9).

In this repo

The depth numbers on each box above are computed once per patched layer, in add_lora_to_unet, src/model.py:254-259:

if "mid_block" in path:      depth = self.max_depth
elif "down_blocks" in path:  depth = int(path.split("down_blocks.")[1][0])
elif "up_blocks" in path:    depth = self.max_depth - int(path.split("up_blocks.")[1][0])

Notice up_blocks depth is mirrored (max_depth - N) — because up_blocks.0 sits right after the bottleneck (depth 3) and up_blocks.3 produces the final full-resolution output (depth 0). This depth number is exactly how each patched layer later knows which of the mapper's 4 outputs belongs to it (Lesson 9).

05Architecture

How the text prompt gets in

Concept

Inside each resolution stage (except the deepest) sits an attention block. Attention lets every "pixel" of the current feature grid look at every word of the prompt and ask "how relevant are you to me right now?" — this is called cross-attention, because it's the image attending to text, not to itself. This is the only place text influences the UNet.

Mechanically, attention has query/key/value projections; the two that matter here are called to_k and to_v — they're the layers that actually read the prompt's embeddings. Remember those two names; they come back in Lesson 8 as the alternate target for a different kind of LoRA (style, not structure).

In this repo

prompt_embeds (CLIP's encoding of your prompt, src/model.py:462) get handed straight to the UNet call from Lesson 3:

self.unet(noisy_latents, timesteps, encoder_hidden_states=prompt_embeds)

cross_attention_dim: 768 in unet/config.json confirms this: every cross-attention block expects a 768-number-wide text embedding to read from.

06Adaptation

Plain LoRA (no diffusion involved yet)

Concept

Say a giant, frozen weight matrix W sits inside a network, and you want to adapt its behavior without touching its (huge, expensive) weights. Trick: leave W completely alone, and add a parallel, much smaller path next to it:

output = W(x)  +  B(A(x))

A shrinks x down to a tiny "rank" (say 128 numbers instead of 1280), B expands it back up. A and B together have far fewer numbers than W, so they're cheap to train and cheap to save. You train only A/B; W never moves. This is standard LoRA — nothing repo-specific yet.

In this repo

The entire pretrained UNet is frozen before any LoRA is even added — src/model.py:124:

self.unet.requires_grad_(False)

Then SimpleLoraLinear and LoRAConv in src/lora.py build close to the textbook form above — self.W frozen (requires_grad_(False) right after creation), self.A/self.B trainable.

07The core idea

Making the LoRA conditional (FiLM)

Concept

Plain LoRA's B(A(x)) behaves identically no matter which image you're generating — a fixed, static edit. What if instead you want the edit to change depending on some extra input — "bend the output towards matching THIS segmentation layout"? The correction needs to be a function of that extra signal, not a constant.

The standard trick is called FiLM (Feature-wise Linear Modulation): compute a per-pixel scale and shift from your conditioning signal, and apply them as scale × activation + shift somewhere inside the network. Two small layers, conventionally called gamma (→ scale) and beta (→ shift), turn your conditioning map into those numbers.

This one idea — modulate the LoRA's internal activation using a conditioning signal, instead of leaving it alone — is the entire novelty of this paper. Everything else you've read so far (frozen weight, low-rank path, UNet shape) is standard machinery this idea gets bolted onto.

In this repo

NewStructLoRAConv.forward(), src/lora.py:177-197 — the class used for structure conditioning (depth today, your segmentation encoder on this branch):

w_out = self.W(x)                                   # frozen conv, untouched
c = self.data_provider.get_batch()[self.depth]       # THIS layer's matching-resolution map
element_shift = self.beta(c)                          # 1x1 conv: c -> per-pixel shift
element_scale = self.gamma(c) + 1.0                    # 1x1 conv: c -> per-pixel scale
a_out = self.A(x)                                      # low-rank down-projection
a_cond = a_out * element_scale + element_shift          # <-- FiLM, right here
b_out = self.B(a_cond)                                 # low-rank up-projection
return w_out + b_out * self.lora_scale

Every other line matches Lesson 6's plain LoRA exactly. Only the a_cond = ... line is new.

08Architecture

Which layers actually get patched?

Concept

A UNet has dozens of conv and attention layers scattered across its stages (Lesson 4). You have to decide: modulate all of them? Only the attention ones? Only the first conv of each block? This choice is a config knob, not something baked permanently into the class — the same conditional-LoRA class from Lesson 7 gets stamped onto however many layers you choose.

In this repo

add_lora_to_unet(), src/model.py:138, walks every named weight in the UNet and string-matches its path against a config's adaption_mode. Structure conditioning (configs/lora/struct.yaml) uses:

adaption_mode: only_res_conv     # matches paths containing "0.conv1" or "1.conv1"
lora_cls: NewStructLoRAConv      # the class from Lesson 7
c_dim: 128                       # width of the conditioning signal each layer reads
rank: 128                        # size of A/B's bottleneck

Every matching layer is physically swapped, model.py:313: setattr(parent_module, target_name, lora) — the original Conv2d is replaced in the live UNet by a NewStructLoRAConv wrapping that same frozen weight plus the new FiLM path. Style conditioning (a single global CLIP image vector, configs/lora/style.yaml) uses the same skeleton on attn2's to_k/to_v instead (adaption_mode: only_cross, lora_cls: SimpleLoraLinear) — worth knowing it exists, not what your segmentation work touches.

09The delivery pipeline

Encoder → Mapper → DataProvider

Concept

You start with one raw conditioning image. Three separate jobs happen before it can reach the c in Lesson 7:

  • 1. Turn the raw image into a canonical map. A depth map, a segmentation map — something meaningful, same size as the input.
  • 2. Reshape that map into every resolution the UNet's stages live at. Lesson 4 showed 4 stages at 4 sizes (64/32/16/8). One flat map is the wrong shape for all of them — you need 4 versions.
  • 3. Deliver the correctly-sized version to each patched layer, since a layer at the 64×64 stage needs the 64×64 version, not the 8×8 one.

Three jobs, three small components: an encoder (job 1 — possibly pretrained, possibly frozen), a mapper (job 2 — a small trainable CNN purpose-built for this repo), and a DataProvider (job 3 — a shared mailbox, deliberately dumb).

In this repo

One forward pass through this pipeline, src/model.py:519-537:

cond = encoder(lora_c)          # raw image -> canonical map [B,3,512,512]
mapped_cond = mapper(cond)      # -> FOUR tensors, one per UNet stage
dp.set_batch(mapped_cond)       # DataProvider now holds all 4

DataProvider itself, src/utils.py:277, is intentionally dumb — set_batch / get_batch, nothing else:

def set_batch(self, batch): self.batch = batch
def get_batch(self): return self.batch

And FixedStructureMapper15, src/mapper_network.py:18, is a plain downsampling CNN — halves the 512×512 map repeatedly (64→32→16→8) with a 1×1 conv "tap" at each stop producing that stage's output. Four taps, four outputs, matched 1:1 to Fig. 2.

10Why your branch works

Why the SegFormer swap is (almost) free

Concept

Look again at job 1 from Lesson 9 — the encoder. Nothing downstream (mapper, DataProvider, patched layers) cares what kind of canonical map it receives, only its shape and value range. If two different encoders both produce "[B, 3, 512, 512], values 0 to 1," they are interchangeable from the mapper's point of view — that's the whole point of Fig. 4 drawing MiDaS and SegFormer as two inputs into the same box.

In this repo

The encoder slot's contract, confirmed from how midas is called at model.py:535:

  • input: [B, 3, H, W] float in [-1, 1]
  • output: [B, 3, 512, 512] float in [0, 1]

DepthEstimator (MiDaS) and your SegmentationEncoder (src/encoders/seg_encoder.py, wrapping nvidia/segformer-b5-finetuned-cityscapes-1024-1024, 19-class Cityscapes colour palette) both satisfy it. That's why the segformer branch only needed one new file plus a mirrored config (configs/lora/encoder/segformer.yaml next to midas.yaml) — the mapper (Lesson 9), the FiLM math (Lesson 7), and the layer-targeting (Lesson 8) are all encoder-agnostic by construction. You weren't integrating segmentation into the architecture so much as handing the existing architecture a different-flavored 512×512×3 map.

Resolution — updated 2026-08, this note was wrong before: your 1280×800 images need a target divisible by 64 before they reach the encoder — confirmed exactly right by Fig. 1/Fig. 2's math (VAE ÷8, UNet ÷8 more = ÷64 total). This repo used to letterbox-pad to a 512×512 square — but a real checkpoint grid (AM.jpeg) showed the model had learned the pad band as real scene content, visible in the input, the conditioning map, and the generated output alike. Padding wasn't a cosmetic side-effect; it was training data. Fixed by switching to resize_mode: "aspect" — a direct resize to 512×320, a non-square target matching the source's real 1.6:1 ratio exactly (1280/800 = 512/320 = 1.6), still divisible by 64, with no pad and nothing spurious to learn. 512×320 rather than a larger same-ratio target like 832×512: it caps the long side at exactly SD1.5's native 512, the safer choice against the known duplication-artifact risk of pushing the base model past its trained scale.
Base checkpoint — also updated 2026-08: stock SD1.5 swapped for epiCRealism, a photorealistic finetune of the same architecture — same UNet, same state-dict keys, so every lesson above still applies unchanged; only the frozen weights inside W (Lesson 6) differ. Paired with the sd-vae-ft-mse VAE via a new model.vae_path config. Two more no-retrain levers now exist for fighting the "fits the shape but doesn't know how it looks" symptom from Lesson 7: negative_prompt (steer away from a specific bad pattern, e.g. garbled road markings) and the already-built lora_scale_start/end decay (let late denoising steps lean on the base model's own prior instead of full-strength FiLM conditioning throughout). None of this moves the architecture's ceiling — see the honest note in Further Study below for what does and doesn't change that.
11Maintainer audit

Verified, not assumed: a full architectural pass

Concept

Everything in Lessons 1–10 explains how the pieces are supposed to fit together. This lesson is different: it's the record of actually checking, by reading the real code and running it, that they do — Config B is really what's configured, the mapper's four output resolutions really do land on the UNet's four stages, the seg map and RGB image really are the same size, and the text prompt really does reach the model instead of silently getting dropped somewhere in this fork's changes.

In this repo

Configuration B, confirmed. configs/lora/struct.yaml and configs/experiment/train_seg.yaml both set adaption_mode: only_res_conv, rank: 128, c_dim: 128 — the paper's "adapt the first conv of every ResNet block" configuration, targeting paths containing 0.conv1/1.conv1 in add_lora_to_unet. Not Configuration A (only_self, attention-based) — that variant exists in this repo but is wired only into an old depth ablation, never this branch.

UNet ↔ mapper spatial alignment, confirmed by direct computation. FixedStructureMapper15 (Lesson 9) is pure stride-2 convolution — no fixed-size assumption baked in. For this branch's real 512×320 (W,H) training target: the seg map starts at (H=320,W=512), the mapper's self.down (3× stride-2) produces (40,64) — exactly the VAE's ÷8 latent size — and block1/block2/block3 each halve again to (20,32), (10,16), (5,8), landing exactly on down_blocks/mid_block's four stages from Fig. 2. Because both the RGB (via the VAE) and the seg map (via the mapper) start from the same size config value and halve in lockstep, this holds for any size divisible by 64 — not a coincidence specific to 512×320.

Image/seg size match, prompt usage, and config wiring, all traced through real code and a real Hydra compose:

  • SegJsonDataModule.__init__ builds the RGB transform and constructs SegJsonDataset from the exact same size variable — no second copy that could drift.
  • The main training loop's loss call uses batch["caption"] (the real per-image prompt) whenever no global override is set — traced into SD15.get_input(), which tokenizes it and runs it through the real CLIP text encoder, same as stock SD1.5.
  • hydra.compose() on both train_seg.yaml and inference_seg.yaml resolves cleanly end-to-end — every interpolation (${size}, ${resize_mode}) lands on the same value across model, encoder, and data config, checked by running the actual compose, not by reading it and assuming.

mIoU (src/utils.py compute_miou) is the standard per-class intersection-over-union, averaged only over classes present in either map — read the full implementation and its call site in segformer_inference.py: both the conditioning map and the re-segmented generation are explicitly resized to the same (size_w, size_h) before comparison, so the shape-mismatch assert inside compute_miou can never silently fire.

FID was a real gap — now filled. torch_fidelity was pinned in environment.yaml but never actually called anywhere in the repo. Added seg_fid_eval.py + configs/fid_seg.yaml: reuses the exact inference_seg model-loading path (same checkpoint, same base model, same VAE) so FID measures the identical model real inference runs, generates N (raw photo, seg map, prompt) triplets, and computes FID between the real and generated pools via torch_fidelity.calculate_metrics. Verified by execution: config composition, the FID library call itself (confirmed against synthetic image folders), and the module import chain all run clean. Not yet verified: the generation loop against a real trained checkpoint — that needs your actual GPU and weights, which don't exist on this machine.

13Simplify, then prove it live

One resize mode, cross-dataset, proven live

Concept

letterbox and CenterCrop were never removed because they were wrong — aspect (Lesson 10's honest note) was already the validated fix for the pad-band bug. They were removed because a settled decision left as a "choice" is still surface area: a config a future run could accidentally select, code paths that have to keep compiling, docs that have to keep agreeing with each other. Once a question is actually closed, closing the door on it is its own kind of correctness.

Removing options doesn't remove the underlying problem they were managing, though — it just means the ONE remaining path has to actually handle every case. That got tested for real this lesson, not assumed.

In this repo

letterbox/CenterCrop deleted, not just deprecated. RESIZE_MODES (src/data/transforms.py) is now ("aspect",) — one entry, not three. _square_rgb_steps lost the branches that built SquarePad/CenterCrop pipelines; every default parameter that used to read "letterbox" now reads "aspect", across seg_map_calculations.py, local_seg.py, both configs, and recommend_training_params.py. SquarePad the class stays — the stock depth pipeline (kept, untouched, per an explicit "keep original-repo files" decision) still uses it — only segmentation's own dispatch logic lost the other two branches.

Cross-dataset resize, actually measured, not assumed. Adding Cityscapes as extra training data raised a real question: Cityscapes ships at 2048×1024 (2:1), not this project's 1280×800 (1.6:1). Pulling a real image straight out of leftImg8bit_trainvaltest.zip and running it through the real aspect pipeline confirmed it — w_scale=0.25, h_scale=0.3125, a measured 25% relative axis skew, visible on inspection as egg-shaped cobblestones and hood-ornament in the output. The fix wasn't to reopen resize_mode — it was a new script, cityscapes_map_calculations.py, that center-crops each Cityscapes image AND its matching label map (identical crop box, so the pair never drifts out of alignment) to 1.6:1 before either ever reaches the shared resize step. Re-running the same image afterward: round again, real content (people, cars, buildings) intact, only empty margin trimmed. The shared pipeline itself stayed exactly as simple as Lesson 10 left it — the fix lives in the Cityscapes-specific ingestion script, where a source-specific quirk belongs.

epiCRealism + sd-vae-ft-mse: loaded and generated, not just downloaded. Both were missing locally all session. Downloaded for real via download_sd15.py, then loaded through this repo's own SD15 class and run through a real 6-step generation at the exact locked resolution — model.pipe(..., height=320, width=512) — on a real GPU. Output image: 512×320 exactly, recognizable street-scene composition. UNet.block_out_channels = [320, 640, 1280, 1280] read back from the loaded model matches Fig. 2 exactly — the architecture math from Lesson 4 isn't theoretical, it's what's actually sitting in memory.

What's still unverified: the 6-step generation above is a load/shape/dtype smoke test, not a quality measurement — it deliberately used too few steps to judge photorealism from. cityscapes_map_calculations.py itself is verified against a synthetic folder tree matching Cityscapes' real naming convention (22 passing checks), not yet run against the real 5,000-image download. Both are named as open, not glossed over.

Further study

No YouTube video exists for this specific paper or repo — checked the paper's own project page and its ECCV 2024 virtual poster page directly; neither links a talk recording. It's a small ECCV paper, not the kind of thing that gets a general-audience explainer. Nothing below is a substitute for reading Section 3 of the paper next to src/lora.py — that pairing is your strongest source.
"Can I swap in Flux / SD3 for better quality?" — no, and it's worth understanding why, once. Flux and SD3 are not UNets — they're Diffusion Transformers (Flux: 19 double-stream + 38 single-stream blocks; SD3.5: MMDiT). No down_blocks/up_blocks/mid_block, so Lesson 8's entire add_lora_to_unet path-matching mechanism has nothing to attach to — not a bigger version of this codebase's approach, a genuinely different one. SDXL, by contrast, is still a UNet2DConditionModel (just bigger), which is why this repo already carries real partial groundwork for it (FixedStructureMapperXL, sdxl_* adaption modes in model.py) — SDXL is a genuine "extend," Flux/SD3 would be a new project. The epiCRealism swap in Lesson 10 works precisely because it stays inside the UNet family; that's the boundary this whole guide's mechanism lives inside.
